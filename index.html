Case studies

Stabilizing a club in financial crisis

Helsinki Athletics Cheerleaders, 2022–2024

When I took on the executive director role at Helsinki Athletics Cheerleaders, the club's account was at risk of going negative mid-season. The previous director had made a one-off payment to avert the most acute situation before leaving. The natural response would have been to cut costs. Before doing anything, I went looking for the root cause.

It turned out the budget had no real tracking system and was built on projected athlete numbers that were not grounded in any data. Nobody had a clear picture of how cash actually moved through the season, most of it arriving in early autumn and January from semi-annual membership payments.

I built a cash flow tracking system that modeled the full season and made the picture visible. Then I changed how budgets were built: based on current athlete numbers and the teams the club would actually run, not on optimistic projections. I made savings where possible without touching the core function. I also restructured the payment model, moving from semi-annual payments to monthly installments. That change smoothed the club's income curve and reduced the financial pressure on member families around Christmas and August at the same time.

The club moved from financial crisis to stable operations. When I left, I handed the role to an internal successor I had identified as the right fit for what the club needed next.

Growing international sales from zero

Sportiro, 2018–2021

When I joined Sportiro, a cheerleading equipment and apparel company, international sales were informal — the occasional order from abroad, handled case by case, with no structured approach to any market. By the time I left, the company was selling systematically across the Nordics and Central Europe with a localized presence in each market.

The work involved more than translating the website. Each market needed its own delivery logic, because the cost and reliability of shipping varied significantly by country and affected conversion. I ran competitor analysis for each market to understand the pricing and positioning landscape before we entered. I adapted the Google advertising to run locally, in the language of each market. And I treated localization as a commercial question, not a translation exercise — the tone and framing that worked in Finland did not automatically work in Germany or France.

The growth was significant. We also attempted an expansion into Russia during this period, which ended when COVID and subsequent sanctions closed that market. That outcome had nothing to do with the approach and everything to do with circumstances, but it was a useful lesson in building commercial plans that account for things outside your control.

Replacing a vendor who was not delivering

AQVA, 2025

Shortly after joining AQVA, I was working with a new chatbot provider that had been brought in to handle customer questions independently and reduce the load on the customer service team. The tool they built was oriented toward lead generation rather than customer support. They had not understood the brief, were not delivering what was needed, and were communicating poorly about it.

The straightforward path would have been to absorb the underperformance and wait. Instead, I made the case internally that the situation needed to be addressed directly. We issued a formal ultimatum with specific deliverables and a deadline. When the provider could not meet it, I pushed for a change.

The case for switching was not difficult to make once the ultimatum had been issued and failed. The harder part was making the initial recommendation to confront the situation at all, particularly when the relationship was new and the change would create short-term disruption. The provider was replaced.
